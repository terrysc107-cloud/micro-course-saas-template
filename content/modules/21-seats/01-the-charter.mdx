A seat is not a prompt. A prompt is what you ask this time. A charter is who the
seat is every time, and it gets read before every single run.

One file. Five sections. This is the highest-leverage document in your whole
board, because everything downstream is that file reasoning about your numbers.

## 1. Identity

Who reports to whom, and what this seat owns. Two or three lines.

```
Rank: Operator
Reports to: me
Owns: revenue truth. What came in, what is about to lapse, what the
      month actually looks like.
```

"Owns" is doing real work. A seat that owns everything owns nothing, and will
produce a survey instead of a judgement.

## 2. Disposition

**The most valuable line in the file**, and the one people skip.

Responsibilities tell a seat what to work on. Disposition tells it how to decide
when two reasonable options conflict, which is most of the job.

Here is a real one, from a marketing seat:

> A retention-first performance marketer. Kills formats and campaigns that do
> not perform, including her own favourites.

That last clause is the whole thing. It tells the seat that consistency with its
own past recommendations is worth less than evidence, which is a genuinely hard
instruction that changes what it does.

Weak disposition is a list of adjectives: *thoughtful, strategic,
detail-oriented*. Those describe nobody and change nothing.

**Test:** does your disposition name something the seat will refuse or kill,
even when it would rather not? If not, write it again.

## 3. Mandate

Three to five numbered things this seat is for. Not a job description. The
things you would be annoyed to find undone.

## 4. Guardrails

Checked every run. Two kinds, and the difference matters enormously.

**Things it must never claim:**

```
- No income or outcome claims. Ever.
- No invented numbers. If a number is missing, say it is missing.
- Never state that something works when it has never actually run.
```

**Things it must never do:**

```
- Never send, publish, or post anything
- Never spend money
- Never change a price
```

Then the sentence that makes this honest:

> **The charter shapes what the seat tries to do. Your permission settings
> decide what it is allowed to do.**

Writing "never send email" is a request to a well-behaved assistant. It is not a
guarantee, because the thing reading your instruction is the same thing deciding
whether it applies right now. The guarantee comes from not giving it the ability
to send. Write both, and never rely on the first alone.

## 5. Cadence

When it runs, and which playbook it follows. One line.

Add this, or you will get padding:

> A run that finds nothing worth reporting should say so in three lines rather
> than manufacturing a report.

## The order matters

Identity → disposition → mandate → guardrails → cadence. Guardrails come after
the mandate because they are the boundary on the job, and a seat should know
what it is for before it is told what it may not do.

## One seat first

You will be tempted to write four. Don't.

Run one seat for a month. You will rewrite its charter twice in that month, and
both rewrites will teach you something that makes the next seat better. Four
mediocre charters written on day one produce four mediocre seats, and you will
not know which one to fix first.

<KeyPoint>A charter is who the seat is, read before every run: identity, disposition, mandate, guardrails, cadence. Disposition is the line that decides close calls, and a good one names what the seat will kill even when it would rather not. And the charter is intent, never enforcement, so write the guardrail and set the permission.</KeyPoint>
