There is a small change in how you write goals that changes what your board
does with them. It is one word.

## The problem with targets

Write *"revenue target: $4,000 this month"* and you have made a subtle mistake.

A target is a thing to reach. Reach it and the question closes. So $4,000
becomes both the goal and the point at which everything is fine, which means it
functions as a **ceiling** — the number where attention stops.

It also gives your board nothing useful to say. "You are at 68% of target" is a
sentence that describes arithmetic, not a situation.

## Floors

Write instead:

```
Revenue: floor $4,000/month. Currently around $2,600.
```

A floor is **the minimum acceptable outcome.** Not the aspiration. The number
below which the month went badly.

That reframes what a report is for. Clearing a floor is not news. Being under it
is, and so is being on track to fall under it next month. Your seat now has a
line to measure against and a reason to speak up.

The instruction that goes with it:

> Report distance to the floor and whether we will clear it. Never report
> percent to target.

## Where this came from

This is not a theory. It came out of a real board meeting where the owner said
that hard numeric goals were reading as ceilings, and every target was
reconverted to a minimum in the same session. It then propagated into every
goals file and every playbook, because once you see it you cannot write a target
again.

You are getting it on day one. That is most of what a case study is for.

## Why your board says everything is fine

This is the most common failure in the whole system and it is almost always
this.

Run after run comes back broadly positive. Nothing is flagged. It feels like the
board is agreeable or the model is weak.

It is neither. **Without floors, no number can be off track**, because there is
no line to be under. Everything is fine because nothing is capable of being not
fine.

If your board has never flagged anything, stop reading and go write floors. That
is the fix, and it takes ten minutes.

## Say what you are not doing

A goals file should also carry the things you have ruled out for the period:

```
## What I am NOT doing this quarter
- No new service lines. Depth over breadth until revenue is stable.
- No paid ads until organic enquiries clear the floor.
```

Without this, a seat will keep proposing the sensible-looking thing you already
considered and rejected, and you will spend a portion of every review
re-deciding a settled question. This section is cheap and it saves that.

## Set floors you would actually accept

The temptation is to set a floor that is really an aspiration, because a low
number feels like low ambition.

Resist it. A floor you miss every month stops meaning anything, and your board
starts opening every report with a failure you have learned to ignore. That is
worse than no floor, because now you are training yourself to skip the top of
the report.

Set the number where you would honestly say the month did not go well. If that
number is uncomfortably low right now, that is information.

<KeyPoint>A target is a ceiling with better branding. A floor is the minimum you would accept, so clearing it is silence and missing it is the headline. If your board never flags anything, it almost certainly has no floors, and that is a ten-minute fix.</KeyPoint>
