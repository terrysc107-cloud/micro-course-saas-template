Your board's output is a report you read once a week. This lesson is what goes
in it, and more usefully, what does not.

## The shape

Nine sections. You will not use all of them in week one, and that is fine.

```
1. Headline          one paragraph, the single most important thing
2. Metrics           the table, each number against its floor
3. Freshness         what was re-checked, what drifted
4. What moved        shipped, drafted, decided. short.
5. Goals             distance to each floor, will we clear it
6. Asks              MAXIMUM THREE, ordered by what they unblock
7. Drafts            things written and deliberately not sent
8. Next week         one paragraph
9. Promotion status  rank, requesting or not, and why
```

The interesting parts are 1, 6 and 9. The rest is bookkeeping.

## The headline rule

Left alone, a seat will lead with the most interesting thing that happened. That
is usually the wrong choice, because the most interesting thing is rarely the
most important.

So give it a standing rule:

> A scheduled run that has died, or a number we previously published that turned
> out to be wrong, is the headline. Above anything good that happened.

This one instruction changes the character of every report you get. It means bad
news arrives at the top while it is still cheap, rather than in paragraph nine
where you will not read it.

A real board following this rule opened a report with *"the daily run is still
dead, last entry 40 days ago"* — above a week in which real things shipped. That
is the rule working. It is uncomfortable, which is the point.

## Three asks, and why the cap matters

Section 6 is the only part that produces action, and it is the part most likely
to bloat.

Cap it at three. Order them by **what they unblock**, not by how hard they are.

The cap is not tidiness. A report that promises a five-minute read cannot hand
over seven decisions; you will skim it, pick the easy one, and the other six
will roll silently into next week where they will be joined by five more. Three
things you actually decide beats seven you defer.

A seat running the same board noticed it was handing over six or seven and wrote
this into its own goals: *"I have been handing over 6–7 when the charter
promises 5 minutes."* Then it capped itself. That is the system working on
itself, which is what section 9 exists to encourage.

## Say what you could not check

The section everyone omits, and the one that makes a report trustworthy:

```
Source: read-only query pack. Stripe dashboard NOT pulled this session,
so the dollar figures are the in-app ledger only. No traffic source is
connected, so acquisition is unanswerable.
```

That paragraph does more for your confidence in the other numbers than any of
the numbers themselves. A report that names its gaps is telling you where its
edges are. A report with no gaps is either perfect or not looking.

## If reports are getting longer

Watch for this. It happens to everyone around week five.

Length creep almost always means the report is drifting from **decisions** toward
**recap**, and recap is the part you were already there for. You lived the week.

The fix is one line in the playbook:

```
Do not restate the week. I was there.
```

## Write the template once

Put the nine sections in `BOARD-MEETINGS/TEMPLATE.md` and have the playbook say
"use the template." Then the shape stops being something the seat re-invents
each week, and you get reports you can compare to each other.

Comparability is underrated. Two reports in the same shape let you see a trend.
Two reports in different shapes just look like two reports.

<KeyPoint>Nine sections, but the work is in three: a headline governed by a standing rule that puts bad news on top, asks capped at three and ordered by what they unblock, and an honest statement of what could not be checked. Growing reports mean recap is creeping in.</KeyPoint>
