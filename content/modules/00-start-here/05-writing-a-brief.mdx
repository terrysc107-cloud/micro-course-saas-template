The gap between people who get a lot out of this and people who get very little
is almost never the tool. It is the brief.

"Give me a marketing plan for my business" gets you something that reads well
and could be about anyone. Here is the version that does not.

## Four parts

**1. The job.** One sentence. What is this run for?

**2. The inputs.** Which of your files it should read. Name them.

**3. The output shape.** What you want back, and how long.

**4. The boundaries.** What it must not do.

## The same ask, both ways

Vague:

```
Help me figure out what to do about my marketing.
```

Briefed:

```
Job: tell me what to publish in the next two weeks.

Read: GOALS.md for what I am trying to move, METRICS.md for where traffic
and enquiries actually stand, and DECISION-LOG.md so you do not suggest
something I have already ruled out.

Give me: three options, each one sentence, each naming which number in
METRICS.md it is meant to move and roughly how long it takes me.

Do not: write any of the content, suggest paid ads, or recommend a channel
I am not already on. If a number you need is missing, say so rather than
estimating.
```

The second one takes ninety seconds longer to write and is the difference
between output you can act on and output you skim once.

## The part people skip

**Boundaries.** Almost everyone writes the first three and stops.

Without them you get a marketing plan, plus a suggested rebrand, plus an
observation about your pricing, plus a content calendar you did not ask for.
None of it wrong exactly, all of it unrequested, and the thing you actually
needed is buried in the middle.

"Do not write the content" is worth more than any amount of describing what
good content looks like.

## Name your files

`Read GOALS.md and METRICS.md` is doing real work in that brief.

Without it, the assistant answers from whatever it happens to have in view,
which might be your files, might be the conversation so far, might be general
knowledge about businesses like yours. The answer will look the same either
way, and you will not be able to tell which parts came from your actual
numbers.

Naming the files makes the answer checkable.

## Ask for a shape you will actually read

"Three options, each one sentence" gets read. "A comprehensive analysis" gets
skimmed and closed.

Be specific and be small. You can always ask it to expand the one option you
liked. Nobody has ever asked a nine-page analysis to be longer.

## Reuse the good ones

When a brief produces something genuinely useful, **save it into a file in your
`ai-board` folder.** Call it `briefs.md` and paste the ones that worked.

That file is the first draft of a seat. When you get to defining a CMO seat,
you will discover you already wrote it, the day it happened to work.

<KeyPoint>Job, inputs, output shape, boundaries. Name the actual files so the answer is checkable against your real numbers. Boundaries are the part everyone omits and the part that stops a useful answer being buried in unrequested extras.</KeyPoint>
