You have a folder and four files. Time to open them.

## Open the folder, then ask a question

Start a session pointed at `ai-board`, and make the first thing you ask a
**question, not an instruction**:

```
Read GOALS.md and METRICS.md. In your own words, what am I trying to move
this quarter, and what is the gap between where I am and the floor I set?

If a number you need is missing, say it is missing. Do not estimate it.
```

Two things are happening here, and only one of them is obvious.

The obvious one: you get a summary. The useful one: **you find out whether it
actually understood your business** before it has permission to change
anything.

Read what comes back carefully. If it describes goals you did not set, the
problem is your `GOALS.md`, not the assistant. Fix the file. That is a normal
and productive first hour.

## The most important sentence in the prompt

> If a number you need is missing, say it is missing. Do not estimate it.

Include some version of this every time.

Left to itself, an assistant asked to analyse a business with a gap in the data
will produce something reasonable-sounding to fill the gap. It is not lying;
it is completing a pattern. But a plausible number looks exactly like a real
one on the page, and you will make decisions from it.

Remember `unknown` in your metrics table. This is the prompt that respects it.

## Now let it change something

Ask for a small, real edit:

```
Add an entry to DECISION-LOG.md recording that I am not adding new service
lines this quarter, and what would make me revisit that.
```

It will not just do it. It will show you the change and ask.

**This is the moment the whole course is built around, so slow down here.**

## Read the change, not the summary

You will see two things: a description of what it intends to do, and the actual
lines it wants to add or remove.

**Read the lines.** Not the description.

The description is generally accurate, and being generally accurate is exactly
what makes it dangerous. It is a summary written by the same thing that wrote
the change. When they disagree, the lines are what happens to your file.

For a board, this is a genuinely easy habit to build, because **the change is
to your own prose**. You wrote `DECISION-LOG.md`. You know what it should say.
You do not need any technical skill to notice that it has invented a reason you
never gave, softened a decision you meant firmly, or quietly rewritten a line
you wanted left alone.

Software people call this reading the diff, and they make it sound like a
skill. On your own files it is just reading.

## Approve, or do not

If it is right, approve it. If it is not, say what is wrong and it will
revise. You are not committed to anything it proposes.

Then open `DECISION-LOG.md` in your own editor and look at it. Not because you
distrust the approval, but because seeing your file change from something you
typed in a chat window is the moment this stops being abstract.

## What you have now

An assistant that can read your business and edit your files, with you
approving every change.

That is already useful. It is not yet a board, because none of it happens
unless you start it. Two lessons from now, that changes.

<KeyPoint>Ask before you instruct. Always tell it to report missing numbers instead of estimating them. And read the proposed lines rather than the summary of them, which on your own prose is not a technical skill, just reading.</KeyPoint>
