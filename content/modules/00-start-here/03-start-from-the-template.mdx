The last lesson had you make a folder and four files by hand. That was the point:
you now know what each one is for.

This lesson hands you the finished version, and something better than files.

## Take a private copy

The board lives in a public starter project on GitHub. You take a copy of it, and
your copy is yours alone.

**Go to the starter, and press the green "Use this template" button.** Choose
*Create a new repository*, name it `ai-board`, and here is the part that matters:

> **Set it to Private.**

Your board is going to hold your revenue, your pipeline, and your customers'
names. Private is not a preference.

## The trap: use the template, do not fork

GitHub offers a second button called **Fork**, and it looks like it does the same
thing. It does not, in one way that matters enormously:

**A fork of a public project is permanently public.** You cannot make it private
afterwards. There is no setting for it, no toggle buried in preferences. If you
take your copy by forking, everything you write into your board is readable by
anyone who finds it, and checking the settings page will not tell you otherwise
because the settings page has nothing to offer.

"Use this template" creates a fresh, unlinked copy and lets you choose Private.
It is the only route that gives you the choice.

If you are ever unsure which you used, open your copy on GitHub. A fork says
*"forked from…"* in small text under its name.

## Get it onto your machine

Two ways, both fine:

- **GitHub Desktop** is an ordinary application. Install it, sign in, and it will
  offer to clone your new copy to a folder you pick.
- **Download ZIP**, from the green Code button on your copy, and unzip it
  wherever you keep documents.

Either way you end up with an `ai-board` folder that looks like the one you made
by hand, plus a few files you have not met yet.

Then open a session pointed at that folder, the same way you did in lesson two.

## Now let it interview you

Type this:

```
/onboard
```

If your assistant does not have slash commands, open the file called
`ONBOARDING.md`, copy all of it, and paste it into the chat. Same thing.

What happens next is about fifteen minutes of questions in four short blocks:
your business and your numbers, your floors, the seat itself, and what it may
never do. Thirteen questions.

It writes your answers into the files as it goes, block by block, so stopping
halfway leaves you with real files rather than a mess.

## What it will not do

Worth knowing before you start, because it explains the behaviour that would
otherwise look like the tool being awkward.

**It will not invent an answer.** Give it something vague and it asks once more,
in a sharper form, usually offering you three real examples to pick from and
edit. If the second answer is still thin, it writes a visible blank instead:

```
<<FILL: when in doubt, prefer ___ over ___>>
> you said: "thoughtful and strategic"
```

That looks like a failure. It is the single most important behaviour in the
whole thing. A blank is visible, and you will come back and fix it. An invented
sentence is indistinguishable from something you actually said, and you never
will, because you will not know it is there.

**It will not say it does not know for you.** "I don't track that" is a real
answer and gets recorded honestly as `unknown`. That is different from vague,
and it is treated differently.

**It will not write a board meeting.** A meeting file dated today would make
your board's own health check report a board that has never run as perfectly
healthy. It leaves that folder alone on purpose.

**It will not schedule anything.** You run the weekly by hand for a couple of
weeks first. You are learning what this seat is like when it is wrong, and you
cannot learn that from a report that arrived while you were asleep.

## When it finishes

It tells you what it wrote, lists every blank that is left along with the
question that fills each one, and gives you three things to do. The first is to
open the charter and change one line, because the first rewrite is where it
stops being a template and starts being yours.

## You can still do it by hand

Nothing here is required. Everything the template contains is something the
lessons teach you to write, and typing them yourself is genuinely not wasted:
you read every line that way.

The template exists because most people would rather start from something that
already works and edit it. Both roads end in the same folder.

<KeyPoint>Use the template button and choose Private, because a fork of a public project can never be made private and your board holds real customer data. Then run the interview: thirteen questions, four blocks, and it leaves visible blanks rather than inventing answers, because a blank gets fixed and an invention never does.</KeyPoint>
