You have the folder, the files, a seat, and a schedule. This lesson is the week
where you find out whether any of it is real.

The instruction is deliberately small: **one seat, five days, one weekly run.**
Then judge it.

## Set up the daily

One scheduled run, early enough that it is waiting for you:

```
Job: tell me what changed and what is off track.

Read GOALS.md, METRICS.md, and PIPELINE.md.

Give me at most five bullets. Each one names the number or the person it is
about. Lead with anything below its floor in GOALS.md.

Do not suggest new initiatives. Do not summarise things that did not change.
If a number you need is missing, say it is missing rather than estimating.

End with one line: the single thing most worth doing today.
```

Two constraints are doing the work. **"Do not summarise things that did not
change"** is what stops a daily becoming a newsletter. **"At most five
bullets"** is what makes it survive a busy morning.

## Update the files as you go

Five minutes, most days. When a number changes, change it in `METRICS.md` and
update the date. When you decide something, log it with what would reverse it.
When someone moves, move them in `PIPELINE.md`.

This feels like admin. It is actually the whole system: a board reasoning over
a stale file produces confident advice about a business that no longer exists,
and nothing in the output will warn you.

If you only do one thing this week, keep `METRICS.md` true.

## Set up the weekly

At the end of the week, one longer run with a genuinely different job:

```
Job: what should I decide this week?

Read GOALS.md, METRICS.md, DECISION-LOG.md, and PIPELINE.md.

Give me: the two things most worth my attention, each with the numbers
behind it. Then anything I decided before that the current numbers now
argue against.

Do not restate the week. I was there.
```

That last line matters more than it looks. Left alone, a weekly turns into a
recap of the dailies, which you already read. Its job is **patterns and
decisions**, not events.

The second request, decisions the numbers now argue against, is the one thing a
board does that you reliably will not do for yourself. You are attached to your
plan. It is not.

## Judge the week honestly

Friday, ask yourself four questions.

**Did I read it?** Not "was it good." Did you open it. If you stopped by
Wednesday, the fix is shorter, not more.

**Did I do anything differently?** One thing you would not otherwise have done
is a successful week. Zero means it is producing correct, unread analysis.

**Did it ever surprise me?** A board that only confirms what you already
believed is either agreeing too easily or has no floors to measure against.

**Did it ever admit it did not know?** If your files contain `unknown` and no
run has mentioned it, it is filling gaps rather than reporting them. Fix the
brief before anything else.

## The three ways this goes wrong

**Too long.** The most common by a distance. It writes a page, you skim it, you
stop opening it. Cut the bullet count and forbid recaps. Short and specific
beats complete every time.

**Too agreeable.** Everything is fine, every day. Almost always missing floors
in `GOALS.md`: with no line to be under, nothing can be off track. Go and write
the floors.

**Stale inputs.** The output looks fine and is quietly about last month. Confident
advice from a stale file is the worst failure of the three, because it is the
only one that is invisible in the output. Only your discipline with `METRICS.md`
prevents it.

## Then, and only then, add a seat

If you read it most days and did something differently at least once, add the
second seat. Pick the area you most avoid looking at, which for most people is
money.

If you did not read it, adding a second seat produces two things you do not
read. Fix the first one first.

## What you have built

An assistant that starts work without you, reasons over files you control,
tells you when it does not know, and hands you something short enough to act
on.

That is a board. It is smaller than the phrase suggests and more useful than it
sounds, and the discipline that keeps it honest is entirely in the files and
the briefs, both of which are yours.

<KeyPoint>One seat, five days, one weekly. Judge it on whether you read it and whether you did anything differently, not on whether it read well. Too long, too agreeable, and stale inputs are the three failures, and only the third is invisible in the output.</KeyPoint>
