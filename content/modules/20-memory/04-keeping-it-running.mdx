Your board runs on a schedule. That sentence hides a requirement nobody
mentions: **something has to be awake to run it.**

If your board reads files on your laptop, the run happens on your laptop. Closed
lid, no run. Not an error, not a warning, just a morning where nothing arrived
and you assume it is still working.

This lesson is the three ways people solve that, in the order you should
actually adopt them.

## Tier 1: the machine you already own

Schedule the run for a time your laptop is open anyway.

If you start work at nine, schedule it for nine fifteen. You are there, the
machine is awake, the run happens. Total cost: nothing.

This is where you should start, and most people never need to leave. A daily
run that fires on weekday mornings while you are having coffee is a working
board.

The limitation is honest: it will not run on the days you do not open the
laptop, and those tend to be exactly the days you would most like a summary
waiting for you.

## Tier 2: something that is always on

Once you are reading the output every day and you want it regardless of your
schedule, you need a machine that does not sleep.

The common answer is a **small desktop left on permanently.** A Mac mini is the
usual choice because it is quiet, uses very little power, and will sit on a
shelf for years. Any always-on computer works. An old laptop with the lid open
and sleep disabled works.

What you are buying is simple: a computer that is awake at six in the morning
so your run happens at six in the morning.

Two settings matter more than the hardware. **Disable sleep**, because a
scheduled job cannot wake a machine that has gone down for the night. And **set
it to restart automatically after a power cut**, or a brief outage while you are
away silently ends your board.

<Callout type="warning">This is the tier where the liveness check earns its keep. A dedicated machine feels reliable, so you stop checking, and that is precisely when a silent failure runs the longest. Keep the check in your weekly review.</Callout>

## Tier 3: being able to fix it from anywhere

Here is the failure that decides whether tier 2 was worth it.

You are away for a few days. The run has not appeared. Something needs a
restart, an approval, or one line changed. The machine is at home, awake, and
completely out of reach.

So the third tier is not more compute. It is **access**: the ability to look at
that machine, and fix it, from your phone.

Two pieces, and they are separate:

**A private network between your devices.** Your home machine is not on the open
internet, and you should not put it there. A mesh VPN, of which
[Tailscale](https://tailscale.com) is the most commonly used, joins your phone,
laptop, and home machine into one small private network as if they were in the
same room. It is free at this scale and it is the piece people skip.

**A way to see the screen.** Screen sharing, or a remote desktop app, running
over that private network. Now the machine is reachable from a hotel.

Between them, "my board stopped and I am not home" becomes a two-minute fix
rather than a four-day gap.

## Do not start here

If you are on lesson four of a course, **you do not need tiers 2 or 3 yet.**

The mistake is buying a machine before you have a board worth running on it.
Start on your laptop. Run it for a month. If you find yourself irritated on the
mornings it did not fire, that irritation is the signal to move up a tier, and
it is a much better signal than enthusiasm at the beginning.

The order is deliberate: your laptop, then a machine that stays awake, then the
ability to reach it. Each step is a response to a problem you have actually hit.

<KeyPoint>Something has to be awake for a schedule to mean anything. Start on the laptop you own, at a time it is already open. Move to an always-on machine when you notice the missed mornings, and add remote access when being away stops being acceptable. Buying the hardware first is buying a solution to a problem you have not had yet.</KeyPoint>
