Your board folder should keep its own history. This lesson is why, and the four
commands that do it.

If you have never used git, that word may carry some baggage. Ignore it. You are
going to use a very small corner of it, and none of it involves programming.

## Why bother

You already have Dropbox or iCloud, which recovers deleted files. This is a
different thing.

**Something other than you is editing these files now.** Your board writes the
meeting, updates the metrics, appends to the decision log. Version history means
you can look at exactly what it changed, in what order, and decide whether you
agree. That is not a backup feature. It is how you keep trusting a thing that
edits your work while you are asleep.

There is a second reason that matters later. When your decision log says a choice
was made in June and revised in August, history is what makes that credible
rather than a claim. If you ever hand this to someone, or sell the business, the
difference between "documented process" and "some files" is exactly this.

## The mental model

A **repository** is your folder, with memory.

A **commit** is a saved checkpoint. You choose when. Each one has a note saying
what changed and why.

That is the whole model. There is a great deal more to git and you do not need
any of it.

## Four commands

Once, to give your folder a memory:

```
git init
```

Then, whenever you have done something worth keeping:

```
git add .
git commit -m "Set my Q4 floors and logged why I dropped the starter package"
```

`add .` means "include everything that changed." `commit` saves the checkpoint
with your note.

To see what has happened:

```
git log --oneline
```

You will get one line per checkpoint, newest first. That list is your board's
history.

## Writing the note

The note is the part with lasting value, and the temptation is to type
"updates" every time.

Write what a stranger would need. Not *what* changed, which the history already
shows, but *why*:

- **Weak:** `updated goals`
- **Strong:** `Raised the revenue floor to 4k after three months above 3k`

Six months later the first tells you nothing and the second tells you everything.

## When to save

After anything you would be annoyed to lose. In practice that means at the end of
most sessions, and always after a board run that changed your files.

A useful habit: save the checkpoint immediately after reviewing what your board
wrote. You have just read the changes, so the note practically writes itself.

## Undo

You made a mess of your charter and want yesterday's version back. Every
checkpoint is a point you can return to, so nothing you have saved is ever
really lost.

The exact command depends on what you are undoing, and this is a fine place to
ask your agent: *"I want to restore CHARTER.md to how it was two checkpoints
ago."* It will tell you the command and explain it. That is a legitimate use of
the tool sitting right there, and it beats memorising commands you will use
twice a year.

## Where it lives

Locally, your folder now has history. That is enough.

If you also want it stored online, GitHub is where most people put it, and it
gives you a copy that survives your laptop dying. It is free for this, and if
you started from a template you are already there.

One thing to be careful about: **a repository can be public or private, and your
board contains your real numbers, your pipeline, and your customers' names.**
Keep it private. This is the one setting worth double-checking.

## What you have now

A folder that remembers every version of itself, notes explaining every change,
and the ability to see exactly what your board did while you were not watching.

Four commands. That is the whole of it.

<KeyPoint>A repository is your folder with memory, and a commit is a checkpoint with a note saying why. This matters more than ordinary backups because something other than you is editing these files, and seeing precisely what it changed is how you keep trusting it. Keep the repository private: it holds your real numbers.</KeyPoint>
